How a frog is built

The first time I saw a frog skeleton at a museum I was surprised how little there was. No ribs, basically no neck, and the whole back is only 9 bones. Everything about it is built for one thing: jumping.

Below is what I've learned about the skeleton, the outside of the body, what's inside, what frogs eat, and how a single egg turns into a frog step by step. I tried to keep it simple, the way I explain it on the trips.

The skeleton

  • Skull: wide and flat with big eye sockets. The jaw is long so the mouth opens very wide.
  • Spine: only 9 vertebrae. A frog can't turn its head, it has to turn its whole body.
  • Urostyle: a long rod bone at the end of the spine. It's what's left of the tadpole's tail, and it gives the jumping muscles something to pull against.
  • Ribs: none in most species. That's why a frog can't breathe by expanding its chest.
  • Front legs: short. The two forearm bones (radius and ulna) are fused into one bone called the radioulna. They work as shock absorbers when the frog lands.
  • Back legs: the long shin bones are also fused (tibiofibula), and the ankle bones are stretched out, so the leg has an extra "joint" that adds power to the jump.
  • Hip: the ilium bones are very long and connect to the spine way up the back. This is the lever that launches the frog.

Outside the body

Eyes
They stick out of the head so the frog sees almost all the way around while the rest of it is under water. A clear third eyelid slides across to protect them when swimming. Frogs also push their eyes down into the mouth to help swallow food.
Tympanum
The flat round disc behind each eye is the ear. There's no outer ear, the disc itself is the eardrum. In bullfrogs you can tell males from females because the male's disc is bigger than his eye.
Skin
Thin, moist and full of blood vessels, so oxygen goes straight through it. Frogs don't drink, they soak water in through a patch on the belly. They shed the whole skin every few days and eat it.
Tongue
Attached at the front of the mouth, not the back, so it flips out like a whip. It's covered in sticky saliva that turns thick on contact and grabs the insect. The whole thing takes less than a tenth of a second.
Feet
Water frogs have webbing between the back toes to push through water. Tree frogs have round sticky pads on the tips of the toes instead. Digging frogs have a hard bump on the heel they use like a shovel.
Vocal Sac
Males have a pouch under the chin (some have one on each side) that blows up like a balloon. The air bounces back and forth between the sac and the lungs, which is what makes a tiny frog so loud at night.

What's inside

Organ Where it is What it does Something I found interesting
Heart Middle of the chest Pumps blood 3 chambers, so some oxygen-rich and oxygen-poor blood mixes. Works fine for a cold-blooded animal
Lungs One on each side of the heart Breathing on land No ribs means the frog gulps air: it lowers the throat to pull air in, then pushes it down into the lungs
Liver Big, 3 lobes, right under the heart Makes bile, stores energy The largest organ. It's the first thing you see if you open a frog
Stomach Left side, under the liver Breaks down food If a frog eats something bad it throws up the whole stomach, wipes it with its front feet, and swallows it back
Intestines Coiled under the stomach Take nutrients from food Tadpoles eat plants so they have a very long coiled gut. It gets shorter when they switch to insects
Kidneys Two long dark organs along the back Filter the blood Frogs take in a lot of water through the skin, so they produce a lot of very watery urine
Fat bodies Yellow finger-like strips near the kidneys Store fat They get big before winter and shrink during hibernation

What frogs eat

Small and medium frogs

Flies, mosquitoes, crickets, moths, beetles, ants, spiders, worms and slugs. Basically anything that moves and fits in the mouth. A frog won't eat something that's not moving, it doesn't recognize it as food.

Big frogs

Bullfrogs and Pacman frogs go after mice, small birds, lizards, snakes, fish and other frogs. They don't chew, they swallow it whole, so the only limit is the size of the mouth.

Tadpoles

Mostly algae and bits of plants they scrape off rocks with tiny tooth rows. Some species also eat dead insects, and if food runs out, other tadpoles.

From one cell to a frog

The whole thing takes about 12 to 16 weeks for a common frog. Each stage below has the photo on the left and exactly what the animal is like at that point on the right.

1 The egg (day 0 to day 1)

  • What it is: one single cell, nothing else yet
  • Size: the black dot is 2 mm, with the jelly around it about 8 to 10 mm
  • Skin: there is no skin yet, just a thin cell membrane. The top half is black, the bottom half is pale yellow because that's where the yolk is
  • Texture: the jelly is slippery and cold, like clear gelatin. If you lift it with your hand it slides between your fingers
  • Breathing: oxygen from the water passes straight through the jelly into the cell
  • Eating: doesn't eat, it lives off the yolk stored inside
  • Moving: doesn't move at all

Within a few hours the single cell splits into 2, then 4, then 8, then 16, 32, 64 and keeps doubling. By the end of the first day it's a hollow ball of a few thousand tiny cells, but it still looks like the same black dot from the outside. The black side always faces up so it soaks up heat from the sun, and the jelly keeps it from drying out and makes it hard for fish to swallow.

2 The embryo (day 1 to about week 2)

  • What it is: a ball of cells that is turning into a body
  • Size: grows from 2 mm to about 5 or 6 mm long, still inside the jelly
  • Skin: the first real skin appears, only one or two cells thick, dark brown, soft and see-through
  • Texture: still inside the jelly so it feels the same, slippery and wet
  • Breathing: through the whole surface of the body, no gills yet
  • Eating: still the yolk, which is now in its belly
  • Moving: near the end it twitches and wriggles inside the egg

The ball of cells folds inward and the cells sort themselves into layers: the outside layer becomes skin and nerves, the middle becomes muscle, bone and heart, the inside becomes the gut. A groove along the back closes into a tube, that's the spine and brain. The body stretches into a comma shape with a head end and a tail end. Around day 4 or 5 the heart starts beating and you can see the tail bud. Warm water makes this go faster, cold water slows it down.

3 Just hatched (week 2 to 3)

  • What it is: a hatchling, the very first tadpole stage
  • Size: 6 to 9 mm, about the length of a grain of rice
  • Skin: almost black, thin and a little see-through, covered with a slimy mucus layer
  • Texture: soft and slippery, you can't hold it, it slides off your finger
  • Breathing: feathery gills sticking out on both sides of the head, plus through the skin
  • Eating: first the leftover yolk, then after a few days it scrapes algae and the old jelly
  • Moving: barely. It hangs onto plants with a sticky spot under its chin and wriggles

The tadpole breaks out of the jelly using a special gland on its snout that dissolves the egg. For the first few days it just hangs there on the jelly or a leaf. The mouth isn't open yet and the eyes don't work. The outside gills are the thing to look for, they look like tiny red feathers. Hundreds hatch at the same time and they stay in a group, that's the black cloud you see at the edge of a pond in spring.

4 The tadpole (week 3 to 6)

  • What it is: a full tadpole, basically a swimming stomach with a tail
  • Size: 1.5 to 3.5 cm, most of it tail
  • Skin: smooth, speckled brown with gold or bronze dots, the belly is lighter and you can see the coiled gut through it
  • Texture: slimy and smooth, feels like a wet grape. The tail fin is thin like plastic wrap
  • Breathing: the outside gills are now covered by skin. Water goes in the mouth, over the inside gills, and out a small hole on the left side
  • Eating: algae and soft plants, scraped off with a hard beak and tiny rows of teeth around the mouth
  • Moving: swims by whipping the tail side to side

This is the growing stage. It eats all day and gets bigger every day. The gut is a long spiral because plant food is hard to digest. The eyes are on the sides of the head. Along the body there is a line of tiny sensors that feel movement in the water, the same thing fish have. The skeleton is still soft cartilage, not real bone. If the pond is crowded or drying up, tadpoles release a chemical that makes the others grow faster.

5 Back legs (week 6 to 9)

  • What it is: a tadpole that has started changing into a frog
  • Size: 3 to 4 cm, the biggest it will ever be as a tadpole
  • Skin: getting thicker, and the first dark spots of the adult pattern show up on the back legs
  • Texture: still slimy but firmer, and the legs feel like soft rubber
  • Breathing: gills and skin, but the lungs are growing inside, so it starts swimming up to gulp air from the surface
  • Eating: still mostly algae, but it begins picking at dead insects and even dead tadpoles
  • Moving: still swims with the tail, the legs just trail behind for now

Two little bumps appear where the tail meets the body. Over about two weeks they stretch into legs, the toes separate, and webbing grows between them. All of this is started by the thyroid gland, which releases a hormone called thyroxine that tells every part of the body to change. If a tadpole has no thyroid it just stays a tadpole forever and keeps growing.

6 Front legs and a new face (week 10 to 12)

  • What it is: half tadpole, half frog, with all four legs and a tail
  • Size: the body is about 1.5 cm, the tail shrinks from 2 cm to nothing in a few days
  • Skin: changes from tadpole skin to frog skin: thicker, with mucus glands, brown or olive, and the dark mask behind the eye appears
  • Texture: moist but no longer slimy, it feels like wet leather
  • Breathing: the gills close up, now it uses the lungs and the skin
  • Eating: nothing. It stops eating completely during this part and lives off the tail
  • Moving: swims with the back legs, and starts crawling out onto floating leaves

The front legs were already growing inside under the skin. The left one pops out through the breathing hole, the right one breaks through the skin. Then everything changes at once: the little sucking mouth with the beak falls off and a wide frog mouth grows, a tongue forms, the eyes bulge up and move to the top of the head, and the gut gets much shorter because it's switching from plants to insects. The tail doesn't drop off, the body digests it cell by cell from the inside and uses it as food.

7 The froglet (week 12 to 16)

  • What it is: a tiny complete frog
  • Size: 1 to 1.5 cm, it can sit on your fingernail
  • Skin: smooth, moist, brown or olive with dark blotches, very thin and it dries out in minutes if it leaves the damp grass
  • Texture: cool, damp and soft, like touching a peeled grape
  • Breathing: lungs on land, and through the skin as long as the skin is wet
  • Eating: tiny live insects: fruit flies, springtails, aphids, small ants. It only eats things that move
  • Moving: jumps. The back legs are now longer than the whole body

Once the tail is gone it climbs out of the pond, usually on a rainy night in early summer, thousands at the same time. It has to stay in wet grass and under leaves or it dies from drying out. Only about 1 in 50 froglets survives the first year, birds, fish, snakes and even bigger frogs eat them. It grows slowly from here, a few millimetres a month.

8 The adult (2 to 3 years old)

  • What it is: a full grown frog ready to breed
  • Size: 6 to 9 cm body, females bigger than males
  • Skin: smooth and moist, can turn darker or lighter to match the ground, with mucus glands that keep it wet and small glands with a mild poison that make it taste bad
  • Texture: cool, wet and a bit slippery, but firm. Males grow rough dark pads on their thumbs in spring for gripping the female
  • Breathing: lungs, skin, and the lining of the mouth. In winter it sleeps in mud at the bottom of the pond and breathes only through the skin
  • Eating: flies, beetles, slugs, snails, worms, spiders. Swallows them whole, no chewing
  • Moving: jumps up to 20 times its body length, and swims with the webbed back feet

It sheds its whole skin about once a week and eats it. Every spring the adults walk back to the exact pond they were born in, sometimes a kilometre away, following smell and the sound of other frogs. The males sit at the edge and call with the vocal sac, the female lays the eggs, and the whole thing starts again from a single cell. A wild common frog lives about 8 years.
